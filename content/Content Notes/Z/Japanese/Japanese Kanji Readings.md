# Japanese Kanji Readings

The two readings come from the origin of Kanji itself. When the Kanji came from China, there was a Chinese way to pronounce them, and a Japanese word to describe the same concept:

- **On'Yomi**: is for readings that come from the **Chinese** pronunciation.
  - Not _exactly_ the same as the Chinese version, they were derived.
- **Kun'Yomi**: for readings that come from the original Japanese words.

Kanji have at least one On'Yomi and one Kun'Yomi

- ❓Can I have multiple of each?

Sometimes, there is only one reading:

- if  the concept was new to Japan, o ly the On’yomi Chinese exists
- If the kanji was created in Japan, only the kun’yomi exists

More fun: as power changed in China, so did the official readings for the same character with th same concept. Japanese said “por que no los dos”

There were three major reading adoption periods in the history of the Japanese language:

- **Go-on 呉音 (4–6th century):** The Wu Dynasty pronunciation
- **Kan-on 漢音 (7–9th century):** The Han Dynasty pronunciation
- **Tō-on 唐音 (1185–1573):** The "Chinese" pronunciation

Most on’yomi come from the Kan-on period, which is funny because it sounds like “canon”

Multiple kun’yomi readings are possible as well, if the original concept had different words before it was written down

### how to choose readings

- a compound word with only kanji (no hanging kana) is _usually_ read as on’yomi (guessing the most common on’yomi )
  - resemble Chinese, just kanji one after the other
  -

- most single kanji is read as kun’yomi

- There are quite a few exceptions to this

- Kanji+hiragana (okurigana) = kun’yomi

- Mostly adjectives and verbs

For most kanji, there will be a single reading that shows up in 80–90% of the vocabulary.
Learn the rest of readings through vocab.
That way, the reading sticks to the particular edge-case word, instead of the kanji itself
