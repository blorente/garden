# Becoming a Technical Leader - Gerald Weinberg

## Chapter 1

Main ideas:

- Leadership is hard to define, and usually we measure it wrong.
  - It's easy to think of it as "who does the most talking", or some other proxy metric for _doing_ leadership.
  - The author proposes a different metric: Who had the most influence in solving the problem.
  - Remember the Martha Parable.
- It's useful to think in two kinds of systems:
  - Linear systems have one cause to one effect. I reward folks with money, so they do good work. I threaten them with something, I stop doing something.
  - Organic systems are more complex, where an effect is the result of many overlapping causes.
  - People mostly function in terms of organic systems.
  - However, it is useful to think in terms of linear systems. They may not be the most accurate, but they are _good enough_ to allow us to make decisions and take action, and often that's more important than accuracy.
    - Link to [[Crisis Engineering - Layer Aleph]] and the accuracy of map making.
  - Trying to _impose_ linear systems on people tends to be suboptimal. You're trying to mold them into a box, instead of seeing them for who they are and using their best qualities.

### Questions

> Observe someone you consider a leader. How is this person’s life different from yours? Which of these differences are a result of being a leader? Which of them are a cause of being a leader?

\[REDACTED]

> How would you expect your life to be better if you increased your leadership skills? Which of these improvements will arise from your changed behavior, and which from recognition of the changed behavior of other people?

I would expect to have more opportunities to do more interesting work with more interesting people. This would primarily come from a perception of leadership.

It would also help my consultancy. Sometimes, I'm percieved as more of a contractor than a consultant. They hire me because I can _do_ the migration (hands on keyboard), not because they want my advice on how they need to shape their processes. Over time this changes, but it's hard to overcome that initial pidgeonholing. Plus, I myself often slot myself in those shoes through a thousand small actions.

> How would your life change for the worse if your leadership skills increased? Will these changes be worth the rewards? How can you change, yet behave in such a way that these changes do not affect you so adversely?

I would have _a lot_ more stress. I thrive when I have _one project_ that I can obsess over for 3 months. Having more opportunities would indubitably be distracting and stressful, as I'm already experiencing with Bardic Tools and the day job. I can change by learning techniques to set better boundaries and, in general, changing my mindset around the relative urgency of things.

A lot of my personal systems are linear (read: I'm very judgy of myself), and changing that to observe the system as a whole more often would give me permission to not have to be _perfect_ and bat every ball, but just pick the ones that matter.

Do you seek out situations in which your leadership will be positive, or do you more often look for situations in which you can learn to do better? Do you, in fact, learn from these situations, or do you just keep repeating yourself?
